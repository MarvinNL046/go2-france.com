French President **Emmanuel Macron** began a three-country tour of East Africa on Saturday, May 10, 2026, visiting Egypt, Kenya, and Ethiopia to repair France's economic and security relationships on the continent and counter growing anti-French sentiment. This diplomatic push comes as Paris aims to shift from its historical postcolonial dominance toward more equal partnerships, following setbacks in former West African colonies where military coups and expulsions of French forces have eroded influence.

### Background on France's Evolving Role in Africa

France's history in Africa is marked by extensive colonization of large parts of West and Central Africa, followed by sustained political and economic influence after independence. For decades, France was accused of propping up unpopular leaders to secure strategic interests, maintaining a dominant position in Francophone Africa. However, this era is fading. Across the continent, African nations are increasingly demanding equitable, mutually beneficial partnerships, greater control over natural resources, and diversified alliances beyond traditional Western powers.

The tour highlights a turning point, particularly in the Sahel region, where anti-French feelings have intensified amid political instability, military coups, and competition from other global players. In countries like Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger, recent coups led to sharply deteriorating relations with France. French military operations against armed groups, conducted for years, were often seen by local governments and publics as ineffective. As a result, French forces were expelled, creating a vacuum filled by new security partners, notably Russia. Russian influence, including through the Wagner Group and its successor networks, has grown by capitalizing on anti-French resentment.

According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/5/10/macron-tours-east-africa-amid-push-to-redefine-frances-role-in-africa), Macron's visit is part of a broader effort to reinvent France's Africa policy. Since taking office in 2017, he has emphasized replacing traditional influence with genuine partnerships. This includes deeper cultural and educational ties centered on entrepreneurship, climate action, and youth engagement. The initiative seeks to position France competitively against rising powers like China and Russia, which have expanded their footprints through infrastructure investments and security deals.

Macron's itinerary underscores this strategic pivot. He started in Egypt, a key North African partner, before heading to English-speaking Kenya for a two-day "Africa Forward" summit on Monday and Tuesday. This event, co-hosted by France, will gather African leaders and business executives to foster economic and commercial cooperation. Several agreements between French and Kenyan companies are expected to be signed, marking a practical step toward enhanced trade. Notably, this is the first such summit in an Anglophone country attended by Macron since he became president.

The tour concludes in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, on Wednesday, with meetings alongside Ethiopian officials and discussions at the African Union headquarters. Topics will focus on peace and security across Africa, reflecting France's interest in stabilizing the continent while rebuilding trust.

### Challenges and Uncertainties in Redefining Ties

While Macron's approach signals a genuine attempt to adapt, questions linger about its effectiveness. France's influence has undeniably declined, especially in the Sahel, where expelled forces and pivots to Russia illustrate a loss of leverage. Broader anti-French sentiment stems not just from military frustrations but from perceptions of neocolonial economic dependencies. African leaders are prioritizing sovereignty, and partnerships must demonstrate tangible benefits to succeed.

The source notes that Paris is shifting its policy, but persistent doubts about regaining footing remain. For instance, the "Africa Forward" summit's focus on business deals could help, yet it must address deeper grievances over resource exploitation and unequal trade. Macron's emphasis on youth and climate cooperation aligns with African priorities, but implementation will be key. Without addressing root causes of resentment, efforts risk being viewed as superficial.

This tour occurs against a backdrop of global shifts: China's Belt and Road investments have built infrastructure across Africa, while Russia's opportunistic security role fills voids left by Western withdrawals. France's challenge is to offer a compelling alternative that respects African agency.
