# France Shelves Controversial Bill Critics Say Would Criminalise Pro-Palestinian Speech

France has quietly shelved a government-backed bill that aimed to combat antisemitism but drew fierce criticism for potentially criminalising legitimate protest and restricting freedom of expression. The draft legislation, introduced in 2024 by **Caroline Yadan**, a member of the national assembly, has been abandoned after mounting opposition from rights groups, legal experts, and the public.

The bill sparked significant controversy over concerns that it would effectively silence pro-Palestinian solidarity and criticism of Israel under the guise of fighting antisemitism. While the government acknowledged a sharp rise in antisemitic incidents since the Hamas massacres in Israel on 7 October 2023, critics argued the bill's wording veered far beyond addressing genuine hate crimes.

## What the Bill Proposed

The draft legislation sought to widen the existing offence of "glorifying terrorism" by introducing the concept of "indirect incitement," which could be punished even when not explicitly stated. It also proposed creating a new offence for "inciting the destruction or denial of a state."

According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/may/22/france-pro-palestinian-solidarity-silenced-criminalised), legal experts warned these provisions would create impossible standards for enforcement. **Marc Trévidic**, a former anti-terrorism investigating judge, cautioned that the framework would lead to "total arbitrariness," as courts would be forced to infer a person's implicit intent—a task he deemed impossible.

The prohibition on "inciting the destruction or denial of a state" raised additional concerns about fundamental rights. Critics questioned how challenges to the expansion of illegal Israeli settlements or calls for alternative political arrangements could be legally distinguished from the proposed offence.

## Unprecedented Public Opposition

The bill faced extraordinary public backlash. A petition filed on the Assemblée nationale website opposing the legislation gathered a record **700,000 signatures**, signalling widespread concern among French citizens about its implications for free speech.

International human rights bodies also intervened. The **Ligue des droits de l'homme** (French Human Rights League) warned that the text represented a troubling attempt to "shield the state of Israel from criticism linked to its grave and repeated violations of international law." Five special UN rapporteurs issued an open letter expressing concern that the bill threatened "the exercise of protected rights, in particular the right to freedom of expression and opinion, including media freedom."

## The Broader Context

**Caroline Yadan**, originally elected for **Emmanuel Macron's party** to represent French citizens abroad, including in Israel and Palestine, had distanced herself from the president's political movement after he announced France's recognition of Palestinian statehood.

The bill's shelving comes amid ongoing tensions in France over how to balance combating genuine antisemitism with protecting legitimate political speech. French law permits individuals to voice criticism of any country, provided there is no incitement to violence. The government's decision to withdraw the proposal suggests recognition that the bill had overreached this boundary.

## What This Means for France

The withdrawal of the Yadan bill represents a significant moment for French civil liberties. While antisemitism remains a serious concern requiring robust legal responses, the public and international response demonstrated that French society values freedom of expression as a fundamental principle that cannot be compromised, even in service of addressing legitimate social problems.

The debate has left unresolved questions about how France will address antisemitism while protecting the right to criticise government policies and geopolitical actors. Future legislative efforts will likely face heightened scrutiny to ensure they target genuine incitement rather than political speech.
